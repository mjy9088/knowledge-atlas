# Newton to quantum: content and evidence scope

This long-form Korean article asks why successful Newtonian prediction did not by itself supply an adequate atomic description, and what quantum theory changed. Its 16-scene mainline is self-sufficient: controlled classical prediction leads through electron evidence, restricted energy exchange, photoelectric energy per event, the nuclear atom, proton and neutron evidence, discrete spectra, matter waves, and finally quantum-state probability. Six optional two-scene detours add worked reasoning without becoming prerequisites. Each detour returns to its parent scene, and normal mainline navigation skips it.

The work preserves five distinct layers: observation, physical model, deduction, history, and interpretation. It does not say Newtonian mechanics became useless, that Newton alone supplied all classical atomic assumptions, that one experiment proved quantum mechanics, or that authored arrows and distributions are photographs of particle paths. The owned SVGs are independently authored explanatory comparisons. They import no external prose, figures or visual assets; the selected spectral positions reuse the existing sourced numerical claims rather than introducing a new dataset.

## New source consultations and exact limits

The Max Planck Society, de Broglie Nobel lecture, Davisson–Germer abstract, and two APS Physics histories consulted during the first pass are now present in the shared registry under the IDs used by [newclaims.json](newclaims.json). Their duplicate topic-local proposal records were removed. The shared registry was not edited in this revision. All sources below were accessed on 2026-10-20; public access was not treated as reuse permission, and no external prose, figures, tables, data, or code is included.

1. **Max Planck Society, “Energy in packets – What did Max Planck discover?”** The live headings are “The black body” and “The birth of quantum theory,” not the earlier recorded title/headings. The page supports the black-body problem and 1900 chronology, but its concise institutional account is not sufficient by itself to settle what physical discontinuity Planck intended.
2. **Clayton A. Gearhart, “Planck, the Quantum, and the Historians,” Physics in Perspective 4 (2002), 170–215.** Registered as `source:gearhart-planck-quantum-historians-2002`. Printed p. 186 / PDF p. 17 describes Planck dividing the total energy E of N same-frequency resonators into P elements ε=hν for combinatorial counting. Printed pp. 207–208 / PDF pp. 38–39 emphasize that Planck did not state the physical significance of the elements and that his view remains historically disputed. These pages were directly text-extracted and read; this is not immutable-byte verification, a copied primary quotation, or final historical consensus.
3. **OpenStax University Physics Volume 3 §6.1, “Blackbody Radiation.”** Registered as `source:openstax-university-physics-3-blackbody`. The observed spectral maximum and finite ultraviolet behavior, classical Rayleigh–Jeans divergence, and modern textbook quantum-oscillator model supply the qualitative comparison. Its last paragraph states that in 1905 Einstein's photoelectric explanation gave Planck's energy quantum the new meaning of a particle of light; this exact passage supports the date and proposal distinction in `claim:physics.newton-to-quantum.einstein-light-quantum-1905`. Eq. (6.10) supplies the rounded Planck constant used in the two-input wavelength calculation. The textbook's simplified account of Planck's own interpretation is not substituted for Gearhart's printed pp. 186 and 207–208 uncertainty boundary. The SVG is independently authored conceptual geometry, not copied data or a source figure.
4. **Louis de Broglie, 1929 Nobel Lecture, “The Wave Nature of the Electron.”** Printed p. 253 states λ=h/p; printed pp. 254–255 explain crystal directions, diffraction maxima, and electron-diffraction evidence. These printed-page locators were verified from the hosted PDF. The hosted-file revision and translation provenance remain unfrozen, and no quotation is used.
5. **Davisson and Germer, “Diffraction of Electrons by a Crystal of Nickel,” Physical Review 30 (1927).** Only the accessible APS abstract was read. It supports selective reflection/diffraction from nickel and wavelength agreement, not a fresh audit of apparatus, uncertainties, complete data, or interpretation.
6. **APS Physics 18, 23 and 18, 24.** “Quantum Milestones, 1927: Electrons Act Like Waves” and “The Tumultuous Birth of Quantum Mechanics” were read as modern historical syntheses separating the 1924 proposal, 1925–1926 theories, and 1927 evidence. They do not replace primary reports or independent history review.

Existing registered source locators remain authoritative for reused claims. The OpenStax Chemistry 2e §6.2 live page was also rechecked for the classical planetary-atom collapse sequence: orbiting charge accelerates, radiates, loses orbital energy, and spirals inward. This article does not claim to have reopened every source behind every reused claim.

## Worked reasoning and chronology

The photon ledger is explicitly fictional, not reconstructed historical data. With `h≈4.136×10⁻¹⁵ eV·s` and `φ=2.30 eV`, the mainline case `4.00×10¹⁴ Hz` gives `hf≈1.65 eV` and a `0.65 eV` deficit. The first detour case now changes the condition to the exact threshold `f=φ/h≈5.56×10¹⁴ Hz`, where `Kmax=0`; this energy boundary does not guarantee emitted count, collection, or measurable current. The second detour case `8.00×10¹⁴ Hz` gives `hf≈3.31 eV`, `Kmax≈1.01 eV`, and an ideal collector potential of about `−1.01 V` under the stated contact-potential and space-charge idealizations. The mainline obtains `E3≈−1.51 eV`, `E2=−3.40 eV`, `|ΔE|≈1.89 eV`, and `λvac≈656 nm`; the detour compares `6→2`, `|ΔE|≈3.02 eV`, and `λvac≈410 nm`. Both preserve the air/vacuum approximation and energy/brightness distinction. A separate authored comparison applies `λ=h/p` to given momenta `0.1` and `9.1×10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s`, not measured historical electron velocities or a sufficient criterion for classical behavior.

Chronology is deliberately non-teleological: Thomson's 1897 electron evidence, Planck's 1900 ensemble energy elements, Einstein's distinct 1905 particle-of-light proposal, the 1908–1913 scattering/model/test sequence, Bohr's 1913 model, Rutherford's 1917–1919 proton-ejection evidence, de Broglie's 1924 thesis, 1925 matrix mechanics, 1926 wave mechanics/probability interpretation, 1927 electron diffraction, and Chadwick's 1932 neutron inference are distinct developments. Teaching order does not imply inevitability or sole authorship, and the article does not claim an unverified 1920 proton-naming event. The final synthesis SVG is explicitly an authored retrospective: it places thermal/photoelectric, atomic/spectral, composition, and diffraction questions beside later quantum tools without claiming that all branches uniquely required one route or that one spatial hydrogen wavefunction explains blackbody radiation.
